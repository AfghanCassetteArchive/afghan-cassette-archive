A major part of the archive comes from collector Mohammad Hassan “Kaka Zmarai”, who has safeguarded thousands of Afghan tapes in Peshawar while living through decades of displacement. His collection preserves not only recordings, but also vital knowledge about musicians, studios, refugee music networks, and the everyday circulation of Afghan cassettes.

The Afghan Cassette Archive is working directly with him to digitize, document, and support this living archive so that both the tapes and his knowledge remain available for future generations.

Collector biography

A personal archive built one cassette at a time.

Mohammad Hassan Zmarai, affectionately known as Kaka Zmarai, is an Afghan cassette collector, recording producer, and cultural custodian based in Peshawar, Pakistan. For decades, his life has revolved around Afghan music: collecting tapes, identifying singers and musicians, recording artists, distributing cassettes, and preserving recordings that might otherwise have disappeared.

His collection contains thousands of Afghan cassettes spanning popular music, folk and regional traditions, refugee recordings made in Pakistan, rare radio material, private and majlis recordings, cassette artwork, handwritten labels, and information about musicians and recording sessions.

But the importance of Kaka Zmarai cannot be measured by cassette numbers alone. His knowledge of artists, labels, studios, musicians, recording histories, and refugee musical networks often provides context that cannot be recovered from a cassette sleeve or audio file.

For the Afghan Cassette Archive, his contribution is imperative. He is not simply a collection holder. He is a living source of musical history whose memory helps ACA identify recordings, correct incomplete metadata, understand how albums circulated, and reconnect tapes with the communities and musicians that produced them.

Life, displacement & uncertainty

Preserving music while living with instability.

Kaka Zmarai’s archive was built under circumstances very different from those of a university library or national institution. He has preserved Afghan music while living as a refugee in Pakistan, often without the stable storage, funding, institutional protection, or long-term security that formal archives take for granted.

His family’s future in Pakistan has remained uncertain, while changing policies affecting Afghan refugees have made it increasingly difficult to know whether the life and archive he built in Peshawar can remain secure.

The collection itself has already suffered major loss. According to oral-history information gathered through ACA, an earlier shop was demolished and thousands of cassettes were lost. The surviving archive therefore represents not just decades of collecting, but what endured after displacement, physical loss, economic hardship, and repeated uncertainty.

International reporting has also documented the wider pressures facing Afghan musicians and cultural workers in Pakistan as deportations and forced returns increased. For Kaka Zmarai, the possibility of return carries an additional fear: his identity and livelihood are inseparable from music, while musical activity has faced severe repression under Taliban rule.

ACA editorial note: Existing documentation contains differing accounts of exactly when Zmarai first settled in Pakistan. Until this chronology is confirmed directly with him, ACA describes him as having lived and worked in Pakistan for decades rather than assigning a precise migration year.

The physical archive

The shop became an informal archive for Afghan music in exile.

Kaka Zmarai’s collection was not assembled as a museum exhibition. It grew through the everyday circulation of Afghan music: albums purchased from cassette sellers, recordings brought by musicians, copies exchanged between listeners, radio-derived material, private tapes, and music produced within refugee communities.

His shop and recording activities became part of the informal infrastructure that helped Afghan musical life continue outside Afghanistan. Musicians could record, cassette sellers could circulate new material, listeners could find familiar voices, and displaced communities could remain connected to music from home.

This makes the collection especially valuable to ACA because it documents how music actually moved — between Kabul, Peshawar, refugee neighborhoods, cassette studios, private gatherings, and eventually the worldwide Afghan diaspora.

The collection continues beyond older commercial releases. Zmarai has also supported and documented Afghan refugee musicians, including recordings connected to newer waves of displacement. In that sense, the archive preserves both what Afghans carried with them and what they created after leaving home.

Kaka Zmarai × Afghan Cassette Archive

Supporting the collector while preserving the collection.

Photo: Saad Salarzai

The Afghan Cassette Archive is working directly with Kaka Zmarai to make sure this collection is not lost. The goal is not to simply take recordings from his shelves and place them online. ACA’s approach is to work with him as a preservation partner.

Zmarai contributes first-hand knowledge about artists, cassette labels, recording sessions, studios, musicians, regional repertoires, and refugee music networks. In many cases, his memory supplies information that does not exist anywhere else in written form. That makes his contribution essential to the accuracy and historical value of the Afghan Cassette Archive.

Supporting Kaka Zmarai means supporting the archive itself. His knowledge cannot simply be recreated later from digital files. Preserving his oral history, involving him in cataloging decisions, and helping him continue the work are as important as transferring the audio.

01
Practical support

ACA is working to provide equipment, preservation materials, logistical assistance, direct support where possible, and help identifying grants, donors, and cultural institutions that can assist Zmarai and the collection.

02
Build locally

Establish a safe digitization station in Pakistan and train people on the ground so preservation can continue locally rather than depend on occasional outside visits.

03
Digitize

Create preservation-quality transfers of vulnerable recordings while keeping the physical cassette and its artwork connected to the digital record.

04
Document his knowledge

Record Zmarai’s first-hand information about musicians, studios, cassette companies, recording circumstances, and refugee musical life.

05
Preserve for future generations

Build stronger metadata, preservation copies, contextual writing, and long-term storage so future listeners inherit more than anonymous audio files.

An imperative contribution

Kaka Zmarai is not simply the source of a group of tapes. He is one of the people who makes the Afghan Cassette Archive possible. His decades of collecting, recording, identifying, and safeguarding Afghan music — together with the knowledge he continues to share — form an indispensable part of the archive itself.

Why his contribution matters

The archive is not only the recordings. It is also the knowledge required to understand them.

Through his decades of collecting and recording, Kaka Zmarai preserves information about people, places, studios, cassette companies, musical networks, and refugee communities that may never have been formally documented. ACA is committed to preserving both the tapes and the knowledge of the person who kept them.

Archive guide

How to read the catalogue

The archive separates musical style, language, place, format, and recording context whenever enough information is available. Some terms remain broad because cassette documentation is often incomplete.

Mahali

Local or regional music associated with a community, province, language tradition, or performance practice rather than one standardized national style.

Majlis / Majlisi

Music captured or presented in a social gathering, private performance, celebration, or informal listening environment.

Kharabati

A Kabul-centered classical and ghazal tradition connected to hereditary musicians, poetic repertoire, and the historic Kharabat quarter.

Diaspora Recording

Afghan music recorded, released, duplicated, or circulated outside Afghanistan, including traditional repertoire, new arrangements, popular styles, and recordings made for displaced and diaspora communities.

Radio Recording

A cassette containing broadcast material, studio performances, off-air recordings, radio programs, or songs associated with radio circulation.

Bootleg

An unofficial duplication or compilation. The term describes how a cassette circulated and does not diminish its historical or cultural value.

Amateur Recording

A non-commercial or informally captured performance whose sound and documentation may differ from a formal studio release.

Unknown / Incomplete

Many tapes arrived without reliable dates, track lists, or production credits. These gaps are shown honestly and may be updated through community knowledge.

Why cassette culture matters

Cassettes allowed Afghan music to survive censorship, war, migration, damaged institutions, and the disappearance of studios and master recordings. A home copy or music-shop duplication may be the only surviving trace of a singer, performance, arrangement, or broadcast. The archive preserves those objects as evidence of how people listened, remembered, and remained connected.

About the project

Preserving recordings and the lives around them.

The Afghan Cassette Archive is an independent, community-driven effort dedicated to preserving and documenting Afghan music and cassette culture. Cassettes were a primary medium through which Afghan music was distributed, shared, and kept alive—across borders, through diaspora communities, and in living rooms around the world.

The cassettes, audio recordings, images, and related materials presented on this platform are shared strictly for educational, historical, research, and archival purposes. This project operates as a non-commercial cultural-preservation initiative.

All recordings and media remain the property of their respective artists, producers, labels, broadcasters, and copyright holders. We do not claim ownership unless explicitly stated. Rights holders may contact the archive to request updated crediting or removal.
